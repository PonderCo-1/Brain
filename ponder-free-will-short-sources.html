For: Did Your Brain Decide Before You Did?

Sources

The famous experiment
  1. 1
    Time of conscious intention to act in relation to onset of cerebral activity (readiness-potential): the unconscious initiation of a freely voluntary actBrain, 1983 (Libet, Gleason, Wright and Pearl). Supports scenes 1-5: volunteers flicked their wrist whenever they wished and reported the position of a spot of light revolving on a clock face at the moment they first felt the urge; for spontaneous acts the readiness potential began about 350 ms before that reported moment.
Can you still say no?
  1. 1
    The point of no return in vetoing self-initiated movementsPNAS, 2016 (Schultze-Kraft et al.). Supports scenes 8-11: a game in which a brain-computer interface watched for the readiness potential and flashed a stop signal; people could still cancel the movement if the stop signal came earlier than about 200 ms before movement onset.
The noise idea
  1. 1
    An accumulator model for spontaneous neural activity prior to self-initiated movementPNAS, 2012 (Schurger, Sitt and Dehaene). Supports scenes 6-7 and 12-17: when there is no reason to pick a moment, movement may happen when ongoing random fluctuations cross a threshold; averaging those moments produces a readiness-potential-like slope; the model fit real recordings and its prediction about quick responses to interruptions was confirmed in a second experiment.
Choices that matter
  1. 1
    Neural precursors of decisions that matter: an ERP study of deliberate and arbitrary choiceeLife, 2019 (Maoz, Yaffe, Koch and Mudrik). Supports scenes 18-20: people chose which of two non-profit organizations would receive a 1000 dollar donation; the readiness potential was present for arbitrary choices but absent or negligible for deliberate ones.
The brain scan study
  1. 1
    Unconscious determinants of free decisions in the human brainNature Neuroscience, 2008 (Soon, Brass, Heinze and Haynes). Supports scenes 21-22: in an fMRI study of a free choice between two buttons, the outcome could be predicted above chance, but far from perfectly, from frontal and parietal brain activity up to about 10 seconds before people reported deciding.

A note on wording

The water bucket is an analogy for an accumulator model, and the video introduces it with "Picture". The readiness potential is an average over many movements, not a signal visible before one movement. The noise explanation is one leading interpretation, not a final answer, and none of these studies proves or disproves free will; scientists and philosophers still debate what the term means. The brain scan study found hints that predicted simple choices only partly, not mind reading. No real researcher is shown in the animation.
